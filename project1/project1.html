Overview

Settling in shouldn’t depend on luck

The Maharashtrian students at RMIT are undergraduate and postgraduate students, many from Pune, who support each other academically, socially and culturally while they adjust to life in Australia.

When they arrive, the basics are scattered across chats and feeds, the real cost of living comes as a surprise, and meeting other Maharashtrian students happens by chance. I designed ghar. to bring the essentials into one trusted place.

The community

Students from Maharashtra studying at RMIT in Melbourne.

The problem

Advice is scattered, costs are underestimated, and community is found by luck.

What I built

A prototype of ghar., tested with students on Lyssna.

Research

Starting with what I didn’t know

Before any interviews I wrote down everything I assumed, using an empathy map and a context map, and treated each assumption as a hypothesis. My first persona, Siya, was imagined to feel lonely around festivals like Ganesh Chaturthi and Diwali.

I then broke daily life into tasks like finding groceries, building community, finding part-time work and sorting out banking and a myki. Across all of them, students had no single system. Each new problem sent them back to step one.

Five interviews

I interviewed five Maharashtrian students at RMIT who had all been in Melbourne for less than a year. I chose recent arrivals on purpose, because their problems were still fresh instead of softened by time. I trialled the guide on one person first and reworded repetitive questions after feedback.

What the interviews showed

What already exists

A community group exists: the Maharashtra Mandal of Victoria, set up in 1981, says it wants to guide newly arrived students. But its focus is festivals, language and culture, not groceries, living costs or trustworthy local resources.

Official cost figures exist too (roughly $80 to $120 a week for groceries, $35 to $45 for transport, $150 to $400 for accommodation), but they sit across university pages, visa sites and blogs. Facebook groups and WhatsApp chains fill the gap, with the same scattered, hard-to-verify problem I heard in interviews.

Analysis

Two kinds of newcomer

I coded the social part of each transcript into four categories: how people find events, how much effort they put into finding community, how connected they feel, and how they first met other Maharashtrian students. I turned the differences into variables and placed each student on them with a letter from A to E.

Two groups appeared. Students who searched actively and wanted events and clubs became Sanika. Students who put in little effort and let events find them through friends or their feed became Aarav. One student sat in the middle but leaned towards Sanika. Everyone shared the same practical struggles when they arrived.

Mapping the mess

The ecosystem map shows the student in the middle, pulling from a dozen disconnected sources. Family, friends, flatmates, WhatsApp groups and Google or ChatGPT are daily contacts. The Maharashtra Mandal, RMIT Indian Club, LinkedIn and RMIT Student Support only come up occasionally.

The core tension

The problem isn’t a lack of information. It’s the lack of one trustworthy, repeatable starting point, for logistics and for community alike.

Define

Four needs to design for

I wrote four problem statements in the format from class: the user needs something because of an insight. Two come from the personas and two cover new Maharashtrian students more broadly.

Checking I had the right problems

I tested the statements three ways. Asking why led every problem back to the same cause: students have no single place to start. A pain and gain map put each frustration next to what solving it would give a student. For anti-problems, I wrote down how I could make each problem worse, such as hiding costs until after arrival, then flipped those into things my solution should do.

“How might we help new students know where to start and get the basics sorted quickly, on their own?”

“How might we make it easy for students to find their people and plans, without relying on luck or what shows up in their feed?”

Ideate

From sketches to a name

I narrowed my ideas to five features and tied each one to a problem statement and a persona. The checklist and the map answer mostly to Aarav, who wants to sort the basics on his own. The advice feed and events page answer mostly to Sanika, who wants real community and guidance she can trust.

I sketched each feature as a wireflow, so the arrows show the path someone takes, for example from the home page into a short guide on getting a myki.

Why “ghar.”

Ghar means home in Marathi, which fits an app meant to help students feel settled in Melbourne.

Before designing screens I made a mood board. I chose reds, oranges and yellows because they remind me of spices and Maharashtrian food, and they feel warm and welcoming. Click a colour to copy it.

Validation

Did it work?

I tested each feature on Lyssna, with 6 participants per test and 7 for the map. Each test matched what the feature was meant to fix: first clicks for navigation, ratings for ease and trust, and initial impressions for pages that need to explain themselves.

Takeaway

Everyone found the myki step on their first click, and students understood what each page was for. The scores to improve are budget confidence (4.3) and trust in the advice (4.5). One student said they would still do their own research, which is fair.

Reflection

What I take from this

What went well

Starting from five interviews meant every feature traced back to a real need, from interviews to personas to problem statements.

What I learned

The two personas pulled in different directions: one wants community, one wants independence. The app had to work for both.

What I would change

Validate the personas with one of the interviewees, test with more students, and seek feedback every week instead of waiting for submission.
